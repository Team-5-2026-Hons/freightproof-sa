# **1\. Project Context and Objectives**

<a id="_Toc237307363"></a>
## **1\.1 Project Background**

<a id="_Toc237307364"></a>
### **1\.1\.1 Industry Context**

South Africa's road freight sector is one of the largest on the continent, valued at approximately R435 billion annually \(Mordor Intelligence, 2025\) and responsible for transporting an estimated 84% of the country's freight by road \(Cartrack, 2023\)\. The sector underpins supply chains across virtually every industry, making its reliability a matter of national economic significance\.

Alongside this scale, the industry faces a persistent and well\-documented security problem\. South Africa accounts for roughly 95% of all truck hijackings recorded across the EMEA region, with approximately 2 000 incidents reported in 2024 \(SAPS, 2025\)\. The direct financial cost of these incidents is estimated at around R3 billion per year in recovered or unrecovered cargo losses \(SAICB, 2025\), with cargo insurance premiums for road freight reflecting the elevated risk environment\.

Several tools are already deployed across this industry to address parts of the problem\. Telematics systems provides live GPS tracking, geofencing, on truck camera footage, and route deviation alerts for fleet operators\. Parcel management systems manages consignment manifests, warehouse scan\-in and scan\-out, and label and waybill printing, as well as client relations\. The Identity Document Verification Service \(IDVS\) provides national identity verification for registered drivers\. Gate security providers, operate access control systems at logistics precincts, performing licence and vehicle disc scans at entry and exit points\.

Each of these systems addresses a discrete function effectively within its own domain\. The gap is not in the tools themselves, but in what happens between them at the moment of handover\. When cargo changes custody, accountability transfers, and a record is created somewhere in one of these systems but not in a single, unified, verifiable chain\.

<a id="_Toc237307366"></a>
### **1\.1\.2 System Overview**

FreightProof does not replace any system already in use\. Pulsit \(telematics\), Parcel Perfect \(parcel management\), IDVS and the gate security systems each continue to do their own job\. FreightProof sits at the integration layer between them and captures the one thing none of them owns, which is the handover evidence\. This is stated first because it is the most common question asked of the design: the platform is a unifying evidence layer over existing tools, not a competing replacement for them\.

FreightProof is a cargo theft and disputed delivery evidence platform designed for the road freight sector\. Its core function is to record every handover point, referred to in the system as a phase, that occurs during a depot\-to\-depot freight trip, and to produce a verifiable evidence chain from those records\.

At each phase, FreightProof draws on data from multiple independent sources: GPS coordinates from the driver's device, geofence verification computed from that GPS position against the origin or destination precinct, manifest data from Parcel Perfect \(currently mocked pending a PP data\-sharing agreement, as the documented API exposes no scan\-status or manifest endpoint\)\. Fleet\-level tracking and trailer telemetry from Pulsit are a planned additional independent source \(currently mocked\)\. A cryptographic hash of each combined phase record is submitted to the Hedera Consensus Service, a public blockchain, which provides a time\-stamped, independently verifiable anchor for the evidence\. Only the hash, not the underlying personal data is submitted to the blockchain, the design is intended to be consistent with the requirements of the Protection of Personal Information Act \(POPIA\)\.

The scope of FreightProof is deliberately bounded\. The platform does not provide real\-time response capability, armed reaction, or driver routing and dispatch\. It does not replace Pulsit, Parcel Perfect, IDVS, or the gate security systems used at logistics precincts\. Instead, it operates at the integration layer between these systems, capturing the moments of handover that each existing system records in isolation, and assembles those records into a single evidence chain that can be interrogated weeks or months after a trip has ended, in the event of a dispute, insurance claim, or criminal investigation\.

### **1\.1\.3 The Evidence Gap**

A reasonable question is why an industry this large, with this much loss, has not already built a single unified record of custody\. The gap is not an oversight, and it is not a technology problem\. Five structural barriers keep it open, and FreightProof is designed around them rather than in ignorance of them\.

Commercial incentive**,** Pulsit, Parcel Perfect and the gate security providers are independent commercial vendors, several of them competitors for adjacent revenue\. None has a commercial reason to expose its data to another, and each treats its event history as a retention mechanism\. Integration reduces the switching cost that keeps their customers in place, so the incentive runs against unification\.

Proprietary data ownership, the records are not the transporter’s to unify\. Manifest data belongs to the courier principal, telemetry to the tracking vendor under its own terms of service, and gate records to the precinct operator\. A transport operator such as LFG holds the operational risk but owns almost none of the evidence, which is precisely the asymmetry that makes a dispute expensive\.

No industry data standard, South African road freight has no equivalent of a common custody\-event schema\. Each system models a trip differently: Parcel Perfect around the waybill, Pulsit around the vehicle, gate security around the entry event\. There is no shared key, no common identifier for the journey, and no agreed definition of what a handover is\. Any unification therefore requires a new neutral record rather than a join across existing ones\.

POPIA constraints on inter\-party sharing, Driver identity, licence detail and continuous location are personal information under the Protection of Personal Information Act\. Sharing them laterally between a transporter, a courier principal, a tracking vendor and an insurer requires a lawful basis and a defensible minimisation argument for each disclosure\. Full data\-sharing is legally heavy; sharing a hash is not\.

No single party owns the whole journey, the courier principal owns the consignment, the transporter owns the vehicle and driver, the precinct owns the gate, and the insurer owns the consequence\. Each holds one segment and none is accountable for the seam\. Because the losses occur at the seams, no individual party has ever carried both the incentive and the standing to close them\.

These are also the constraints FreightProof accepts as given\. It does not attempt to make the vendors interoperate, to relicense their data, or to create an industry standard\. It creates an independent, hash\-anchored record of the handover event itself, which is the only artefact none of the incumbents currently produces and the only one that does not require the other parties’ cooperation to be trustworthy\.

<a id="_Toc237307367"></a>
## **1\.2 Business Problems**

The following business problems were identified through engagement with our industry partner and informed the scope and design of the FreightProof system\.

1. **Fragmented evidence across disconnected systems at handover points**

Pulsit, Parcel Perfect, IDVS, and gate security systems each perform their function effectively within their own domain\. The gap is between them, at the moment of handover, each system creates a record in isolation\. No unified chain links the verified driver identity, the confirmed vehicle, the signed cargo manifest, and the gate access event into a single queryable record\. When a dispute arises weeks or months later, reconstructing events from disconnected sources is slow, incomplete, and legally fragile\.

1. **Fraudulent drivers exploiting gaps between verification steps**

Driver identity is verified at trip creation via IDVS, but nothing in the current workflow confirms that the same driver presents at the origin gate, departs with the cargo, and arrives at the destination\. Between these points, a fraudulent actor can present a copied licence or forged waybill and pass a checkpoint that verifies only the document in hand\. Because no system links the IDVS\-verified identity at trip creation to physical presence at each gate, this gap is exploitable and has been exploited in organised freight crime\.

1. **Undetected vehicle and trailer substitution mid\-route**

A trailer decoupled at an unmonitored stop, or a driver exchanged at a prearranged point without any formal record, is a direct mechanism for cargo diversion\. On corridors such as Johannesburg to Durban, planned driver exchanges are a normal operational event\. The problem is that no system currently records a substitution as a verifiable, timestamped event tied to the trip's evidence chain, making an unplanned substitution indistinguishable from a planned one\.

1. **Cargo quantity disputes at delivery with no neutral source of truth**

When 100 parcels depart Johannesburg and only 99 are received in Durban, there is no neutral, mechanism to resolve the discrepancy\. The manifest exists in Parcel Perfect and the delivery receipt exists on a physical document, but neither is automatically cross\-referenced against the other or anchored to an independent record\. Manifest data is pulled from the origin or destination Parcel Perfect through its waybill API, not read off a scanned or photographed document, so no optical character recognition step is involved and no transcription error is introduced that all parties \(Load Factor, the courier principal, and the insurer\) can verify without relying on one party's copy of events\.

1. **Inability to produce legally credible evidence months after an incident**

Insurance claims and criminal investigations routinely arise long after a trip has ended\. Physical documents may be lost or altered, GPS records aged out of Pulsit, and warehouse scan logs not retained at the granularity needed to reconstruct a specific handover\. Load Factor and its principals frequently cannot produce the timestamped, multi\-source evidence chain that an insurer or court requires, undermining both prosecution and dispute resolution\.

1. **Elevated cargo insurance premiums driven by unresolved theft and dispute rates**

South Africa's freight theft environment which is approximately 2 000 hijackings in 2024 at a direct cost of around R3 billion per year, has driven cargo insurance premiums to approximately 12\.5% of cost\-per\-kilogram for road freight \(SAPS, 2025\) \(SAICB, 2025\)\. A significant proportion of this premium reflects the industry's inability to demonstrate a clean chain of custody after the fact\. Operators who cannot produce credible handover evidence are indistinguishable, from an insurer's perspective, from those who cannot\.

## **1\.3 Objectives**

<a id="_Toc237307369"></a>
### **1\.3\.1 Business Objectives**

Load Factor Group \(LFG\), our client organisation, operates in a road freight sector where cargo theft, load disputes, and evidence gaps at handover points affect its bottom line, insurability, and standing with clients\.

**Parent business objective:** protect LFG’s margin and contractual standing by making every custody handover provable\.

- **Sub\-objective 1 Reduce Exposure to Theft\-Related Losses:** Lower the direct and indirect costs LFG bears when cargo is stolen or disputed and reduce the operational disruption theft causes to its trips and client relationships\.
- **Sub\-objective 2 Improve Insurability and Reduce Premium Costs:** Cargo insurance is a significant fixed cost of doing business, it accounts for around 12\.5% of the cost per kilogram of road freight moved in South Africa\. Being able to demonstrate stronger custody controls to insurers supports better terms over time\.
- **Sub\-objective 3 Strengthen Client Trust and Competitiveness:** Shippers increasingly favour freight operators who can prove custody and produce evidence in a dispute\. Being able to offer this strengthens LFG’s position when bidding for and retaining higher\-value contracts\.
- **Sub\-objective 4 Reduce the Cost and Time of Dispute Resolution**: Investigations, claims, and disputes that rely on incomplete or single\-source records are slow and expensive to resolve\. LFG’s objective is to reduce the time and cost spent resolving these after the fact\.

<a id="_Toc237307370"></a>
### **1\.3\.2 Project Objectives**

These describe what the FreightProof project itself sets out to deliver, in support of LFG's business objectives above\.

- **Secure the Handover Points:** Close the evidence gap at each phase of a depot\-to\-depot trip, the points where existing systems \(Parcel Perfect, Pulsit, gate security\) each hold a partial record, but none holds the full picture\.
- **Produce Permanent, Tamper\-Evident Evidence:** Deliver records of each handover that cannot be altered after the fact, so that a dispute, insurance claim, or investigation raised weeks or months later can be resolved against a trustworthy record\.
- **Prevent Fraudulent Substitution:** Reduce the ability for cargo to be stolen through falsified paperwork or through swapping trucks, trailers, or drivers’ mid\-trip, by cross\-checking multiple independent sources at each phase rather than relying on any single one\.
- **Deliver a Working Proof of Concept:** The terminology is fixed here and used consistently throughout this document\. FreightProof is being built as a minimum viable product \(MVP\): the smallest complete system that a real operator could run a real trip on, as distinct from a proof of concept, which only demonstrates that something is technically possible\.

<a id="_Toc237307371"></a>
### **1\.3\.3 System Objectives**

These objectives show exactly how the software helps reach the goals above\. They must be specific, measurable, and time bound\. \(Because several of the tools referenced are industry\-specific, a short explanation of what each does is included for readers unfamiliar with them\.\)

- **Secure Record Anchoring:** The platform must create a digital fingerprint \(SHA\-256 hash\) of every pickup and delivery and submit it to the Hedera Consensus Service\. Hedera is a public blockchain used here purely as an independent, tamper\-evident timestamping service, within 60 seconds of a sign\-off\.
- **Multi\-Source Location Check:** The system must cross\-check the driver's phone GPS against the expected depot or delivery precinct before a handover can be approved, flagging a mismatch if the device falls outside an operationally reasonable tolerance \(in the order of tens of meters, reflecting typical smartphone GPS accuracy\)\. Where fleet\-tracker data is available, it is cross\-checked as a second, independent location source\. The exact tolerance is a configurable setting to be validated during testing, not a fixed guarantee, since accuracy varies by device and environment\.
- **Manifest Sync:** The system must poll Parcel Perfect \(the client’s existing freight consignment and manifest system\) during the loading and unloading phase to pull the confirmed cargo list once it becomes available\. The polling interval is 15 minutes during an open loading or unloading phase and stops when the phase closes\.
- **Receiver Identity Verification:** For every delivery stop, the receiver digitally signs by scanning a QR code on the drivers app that opens a confirmation/receipt page in their browser\. They verify themselves with their name, ID number, and selfie to record the receipt of the goods\.
- **Permanent Exception Logging:** The system must pull route deviation alerts from Pulsit \(the fleet telemetry and tracking provider used to monitor vehicle location and movement\) and record them as unchangeable, timestamped notes in the trip history\.
- **Seal Integrity Verification:** The system must record the physical seal number and a photograph at origin gate\-out and automatically compare it against the seal captured at destination gate\-in\. Any mismatch must be flagged as a high\-priority exception\.

### **1\.3\.5 Identity Assurance**

The Iteration 2 review pressed hard on a single question, in several forms: if a driver can hand their phone to someone else, what has device\-based verification actually verified? The honest answer is that it has verified the device and the session, not the human being\. That distinction is stated here rather than left implicit, because several objectives depend on it\.

**What the platform can claim\.** That a specific registered account, bound to a single enrolled device, performed a specific action at a specific place and time, and that the record of it cannot be altered afterwards without detection\. Every phase carries the device identifier, the GPS fix, the geofence result and the evidence artefacts, hashed together and anchored\. If the account was misused, the misuse is attributable and permanently recorded\.

**What the platform cannot claim today\.** That the person holding the device is the person enrolled to it\. Single\-device binding prevents two concurrent sessions; it does not prevent one person handing their phone to another, which is the digital equivalent of lending a staff card\.

**Compensating controls in place\.** Device binding restricts an account to one enrolled handset, so a session cannot be shared across two people at once\. The geofence check ties each action to a physical precinct\. The evidence chain makes the trail attributable after the fact, which is what an insurer or investigator actually needs\. Photographic capture at activation, added in Iteration 3, introduces the first genuine identity signal rather than a possession signal\.

**What closes the gap\.** Live IDVS integration, which verifies identity against the national register rather than against possession of a handset, and driver photograph capture bound into the anchored payload at activation and at delivery\. Both are Iteration 3 priorities \(§9\.3\.2\)\. Until they are in place, the platform’s identity claim is possession\-based, and the objectives in §1\.3\.3 and §1\.3\.4 have been reworded to say so\.

The wider scope point follows from this\. FreightProof is an evidential system, not a preventative one\. It does not stop a hijacking, an impersonation or a collusive dispatcher\. It makes each of them provable afterwards, which is the difference between a loss that is written off and a loss that is recovered, prosecuted or priced correctly by an insurer\.

# **2\. Updated Project and Iteration Plan**

<a id="_Toc237307374"></a>
## **2\.1 Revised Iteration Plan and User Story Map**

The project continues to follow the four\-iteration structure introduced in the Vision Presentation, running from March to October 2026\. However, the scope of the later iterations has been revised as the team developed a better understanding of Load Factor Group's operations and began implementing the system\.

Iteration 1 established the technical foundation of FreightProof\. This included authentication, the dispatcher application, driver and vehicle records, trip creation, the PostgreSQL and Supabase infrastructure, and the first Hedera blockchain integration\.

At the end of Iteration 1, Iteration 2 was expected to focus mainly on a fixed sequence of handovers such as loading, departure, arrival and unloading\. During development, the team found that this approach was too restrictive\. Real freight journeys are not always a simple movement from one origin directly to one destination\. A trip may involve multiple depots, cross\-docking, several consignments, or different cargo being collected and delivered at different stops\.

Iteration 2 was therefore rerouted from rigid point\-to\-point handovers to a more dynamic, leg\-by\-leg chain of custody\. A phase represents an important step in the custody of the cargo, for example activation at the origin, loading, departure, in transit, unloading or final confirmation\. Rather than hard\-coding one sequence for every journey, FreightProof now creates an ordered phase plan based on the stops and consignments belonging to the trip\.

This provides a stronger foundation for real South African freight operations\. For example, a Johannesburg to Durban journey may be a direct trip, while another load could pass through an intermediate depot or cross\-dock facility\. The system should be able to represent both without requiring a completely different workflow\.

During Iteration 2, the team implemented the main components required for this phase\-based lifecycle\. The system can now create and store a phase plan, guide the driver through the phases using the Driver Progressive Web App, capture photographs and other supporting evidence, record GPS information from the driver's device, queue phase actions when connectivity is unavailable, and replay those actions once connectivity returns\. This offline capability is particularly important for routes such as the N3, where reliable mobile connectivity cannot always be assumed\. Note we cannot assign the phone, we rely on what the company already has in use for company given driver phones, data, phone charge etc are already a part of a drivers trip and all drivers are already required to have their phones on and charged at all times\.

The dispatcher can monitor trip progress, view the phase history, cancel an abandoned trip, and override a phase that cannot practically be completed\. An override does not create false evidence or mark the phase as normally completed\. Instead, the missing step and the dispatcher's reason are retained as part of the evidence history\.

Iteration 2 also introduced warehouse scan reconciliation and a Parcel Perfect integration boundary\. FreightProof can compare expected cargo information against recorded loading or delivery information and flag discrepancies instead of silently ignoring them\. A network\-capable Parcel Perfect waybill client has been developed, although the project still relies on controlled or mock data for parts of the warehouse process because full live access has not yet been established\.

The user story map across the four releases can therefore be summarised as follows:

**Iteration 1 Foundation:**
The dispatcher can authenticate, manage core driver and vehicle information, create a basic trip and create the first blockchain\-backed journey record\.

**Iteration 2 Evidence Lifecycle:**
The driver can follow the generated custody phases, capture required evidence, continue working through temporary connectivity loss, and complete the main pickup, transit and delivery workflow\. The dispatcher can monitor the trip and intervene where necessary\.

**Iteration 3 Integrity and Staging:**
The focus moves to strengthening the evidence chain, completing multi\-stop trip creation, improving security controls, ensuring photographs and other important evidence are cryptographically linked to the relevant blockchain records, preparing a stable deployed version of the system, and integrating with live Pulsit and Parcel Perfect\.

**Iteration 4 Pilot Readiness and Polish:**
The final release will focus on end\-to\-end reliability, evidence access, exception handling, POPIA\-related controls, resilience testing, final interface improvements and a presentation\-ready Johannesburg to Durban demonstration\.

The change made during Iteration 2 is therefore an architectural correction rather than a change in the overall project objective\. FreightProof is still intended to provide a verifiable chain of custody\. The difference is that the system now represents that custody chain in a way that can support more realistic freight journeys\.

## **2\.2 Revised Systems Architecture** 

The overall FreightProof architecture remains separated into four main areas: the front\-end applications, the API and application services, the data and evidence layer, and external integrations\.

**Front\-end applications:** FreightProof has two main user interfaces\. The Dispatcher Web Application is built using Next\.js and is designed primarily for desktop and tablet use\. It allows dispatchers to create and monitor trips, view the phase timeline, review captured evidence, cancel trips and override phases when necessary\.

The Driver PWA is also built using Next\.js and is designed for mobile use\. Rather than showing the driver a fixed set of handshake screens, the application now follows the phase plan generated for the specific trip\. The driver can capture photographs, GPS information and other required evidence from the phone\. If connectivity is lost, the application can temporarily queue phase actions and submit them when the connection becomes available again\.

**API and application layer:** The backend is developed using FastAPI and Python\. It contains the main business rules that control how a trip progresses\. The backend creates the phase plan, ensures that phases are completed in the correct order, verifies that the correct driver is acting on the trip, records evidence, handles trip cancellation and phase overrides, and provides trip information to both front\-end applications\.

The backend also provides selected real\-time updates to the dispatcher when important trip events occur\. This allows the dispatcher dashboard to refresh when a driver completes a phase or when an exception is raised\.

**Data and evidence layer:** PostgreSQL, hosted through Supabase, stores the operational information required by FreightProof\. This includes trips, stops, consignments, phases, drivers, vehicles, exceptions and blockchain receipt information\.

Photographs and documents are stored separately in Supabase Storage\. FreightProof calculates a SHA\-256 hash for captured evidence so that changes can later be detected\. Personal information, photographs, GPS coordinates and cargo information remain off\-chain\. Hedera only receives cryptographic hashes used to provide an independent timestamped proof\. This remains an important design decision for protecting personal information and supporting POPIA requirements\.

**External integrations:** Hedera Consensus Service remains the main blockchain service used by FreightProof\. It records hashes representing selected important events and returns receipt information that can later be used for verification\.

Parcel Perfect is used as the boundary for consignment and waybill information\. A network\-capable single\-waybill client has been developed, but the complete live warehouse scanning process has not yet been validated and controlled test data is still used for some scenarios\.

Pulsit remains the planned source for independent vehicle and trailer GPS information, while IDVS remains the planned driver identity verification service\. These two integrations are not yet operational in the current Iteration 2 implementation and will only be presented as live integrations once working access and testing have been completed\.

The most important architectural change since Iteration 1 is therefore the move from fixed handshakes to a plan\-driven phase lifecycle\. The database and backend now support ordered trip stops and cargo roles that can eventually represent more complex multi\-stop journeys\. However, the current dispatcher interface still mainly creates a direct origin\-to\-destination journey\. Full multi\-stop authoring is therefore carried forward into Iteration 3\.

The Iteration 1 decisions to use Hedera instead of Polygon, Supabase authentication instead of Web3Auth, PostgreSQL and Supabase Storage instead of IPFS and Pinata, and to remove unrealistic QR\-based guard workflows remain unchanged\.

## **3\.5 State\-Machine Diagrams**

This diagram follows one Trip from creation to closure or cancellation\. It starts in Trip Creation \(P0\), where the dispatcher's submission builds the phase plan from the stops and anchors the journey\-lock hash to Hedera before saving; this gate is fail\-closed, so if the anchor fails everything rolls back and no trip exists\. On success it waits in Created for the driver to request activation, allowed only if the trip isn't started early, the driver isn't already on another trip, and no earlier trip of theirs is due that day\. Once activated it enters Active, a composite state whose loop runs once per stop rather than in a fixed order: at each stop it asks whether cargo is collected \(Loading, P2\), whether this is the final stop \(Confirmation, P6\) or another drop \(Departure, P3, where the seal is applied and the pickup receipt anchored\), and on arrival whether cargo is dropped \(Unloading, P5\), with In Transit \(P4\) covering the drive until the driver attests arrival\. Loading, Unloading and Confirmation are each held until that stop's warehouse closes its scan session\. Nothing suspends the trip: a critical exception like a seal or parcel\-count mismatch is logged but the trip continues, since the platform records rather than intervenes, and a phase the driver can't complete is cleared by dispatcher override\. The trip reaches Closed once no phase is unresolved and the delivery receipt is anchored, and can be Cancelled beforehand, leaving the remaining phases pending as an honest record of a plan stopped partway\.

## **4\.1 Technical Architecture Diagram**

FreightProof sits across three user types: a dispatcher managing trips from a web dashboard, a driver capturing evidence on a company\-issued Android device, and a consignee who only ever receives a PDF or a view\-only link\. Both frontends talk to a single FastAPI backend over HTTPS, which delegates work to five core services covering orchestration, auth, crypto, storage, and async tasks\. The architecture assumes all personal data stays in South Africa \(Supabase hosted on af\-south\-1\) to satisfy POPIA, and that the blockchain layer, Hedera HCS, receives only SHA\-256 hashes and never touches any personal information\. A trip moves through six handshake states, from creation right through to final delivery \(POD received\), with each transition requiring photo evidence, GPS cross\-referencing via Pulsit, and parcel count reconciliation through Parcel Perfect\. Celery workers sitting behind Redis handle anything that should not block the API, including daily Merkle batch submissions to Hedera, notification dispatch through Twilio and SendGrid, and manifest polling\. The client receives no login in the MVP; evidence reaches them purely by email via SendGrid\.

# **7\. Testing**

This section describes how Iteration 2 of FreightProof was tested\. It uses the same approach and risk categories as Iteration 1, with the test cases updated for the features added since that submission\.

Iteration 1 delivered the foundation \(dispatcher login, driver and vehicle records, single trip creation, the journey lock hash, one blockchain anchor, and the first driver PWA\)\. Iteration 2 built the working evidence chain on top of it\. The main additions tested were:

- Plan\-driven phase engine for a hub\-to\-hub trip \(e\.g\. RTT Durban to RTT Johannesburg\) the system generates the phase chain automatically \(gate\-in, loading, departure, in\-transit, arrival, unloading, closure\) instead of using a hard\-coded sequence\.
- Trip creation with cargo \(waybill\) pulled from Parcel Perfect rather than typed in by hand\.
- Per\-phase blockchain anchoring, phases are fingerprinted and anchored to Hedera separately, with a recorded anchor state and a fail\-safe policy\.
- GPS location tracking and warehouse geofencing\.
- Evidence artifacts \(photos, signatures\) stored in Supabase Storage and read back through short\-lived signed links locked to their trip\.
- A much larger driver PWA: login, one\-time OTP, one\-device session binding, an offline queue, seal capture and verification, checkpoint and exception logging, and a panic \(duress\) alert\.
- Live dispatcher updates over a per\-organisation event stream\.
- Dispatcher trip\-lifecycle controls; a dispatcher can cancel a trip \(a terminal exit\) or override a phase a driver cannot complete, so a trip can never get permanently stuck; both actions are logged to the phase ledger\.

<a id="_Toc237307400"></a>
## **7\.1 Testing Approach**

<a id="_Toc237307401"></a>
### **7\.1\.1 Unit Testing**

Unit testing checks small parts of the system in isolation\. Backend unit tests use pytest and the frontend now uses Vitest; all run automatically before code is merged\. Iteration 2 unit testing focused on the new logic the evidence chain depends on:

- Phase plan generation \- the correct ordered phases are built for a hub\-to\-hub trip, always ending by closing custody, since the trip lifecycle is now generated from these rules rather than hard\-coded\.
- Blockchain anchor payload \- the data prepared for Hedera is built in a fixed, sorted order \(so the same record always produces the same fingerprint\) and excludes GPS, photos and personal details, protecting the POPIA rule that no personal data reaches the blockchain\.
- Journey lock and critical fields \- the system distinguishes a harmless change from a change to a protected \("critical"\) field, so tampering with committed trip parameters is detectable\.
- Driver device sessions \- the one\-device rule: the first login claims the account, a newer login on another device takes over, and the old device is then refused\.
- Offline queue \(driver PWA\) \- actions taken with no signal are replayed once using the same idempotency key; permanent failures are dropped, temporary  failures retried\.
- Geofencing, seal matching and formatting helpers \- distance\-to\-fence maths \(returning "unknown", not zero, when a location is missing\), case\- and space\-insensitive seal comparison that still rejects true mismatches, and consistent date, delay and exception formatting\.

The anchor payload test is a key example: it confirms every phase fingerprint is built identically and carries no personal information \- the basis of the whole tamper\-evidence claim, since that fingerprint must be rebuilt later and compared to Hedera\.

<a id="_Toc237307402"></a>
### **7\.1\.2 Integration Testing**

Integration testing checks that the parts work together, driver app, dispatcher dashboard, backend, database, storage and external links\. Backend integration tests run the real API against a throwaway PostgreSQL database and assert the actual database state, not just the response\. Iteration 2 focused on:

- Trip creation \- a hub\-to\-hub trip records the correct origin and destination, links its cargo, writes the full phase plan, and is immediately advanceable\.
- Walking the phases \- a driver completes each phase in order and closes the trip; an out\-of\-order or wrong\-state completion is rejected; a re\-sent "complete" creates no duplicate\.
- Activation gating \- a trip cannot be activated before its scheduled day, later phases are not date\-gated, and an unknown driver token is rejected\.
- Anchoring and verification \- completing a phase anchors it with the correct receipt type; verification rebuilds a matching fingerprint for an anchored trip and returns "no receipt" for one never anchored\.
- Evidence artifacts \- a trip's photos and signatures are listed in capture order via short\-lived signed links, only to a valid dispatcher; drivers and other organisations are refused\.
- Role and ownership boundaries \- only an admin dispatcher can create or edit fleet records \(others may view\), a driver cannot act on another driver's trip, and unauthenticated requests are refused\.
- Live dispatcher stream \- the stream requires a dispatcher login \(drivers refused\), delivers only the caller's organisation's events, and sends a heartbeat when idle\.
- Dispatcher trip\-lifecycle controls \- cancelling a trip marks it cancelled, keeps its phase rows, and frees the driver to start another; overriding a pending phase requires a note, unblocks the next phase \(closing the trip if it was the last\), and leaves the anchor status untouched\. Both actions are rejected on a closed or foreign\-organisation trip and are recorded as a dispatcher exception on the ledger\.

External systems were again tested with mock or fixed responses rather than live company systems: Parcel Perfect waybills from recorded mocks, and Hedera anchoring through the FreightProof wrapper, so the default test run never depends on the live network\.

<a id="_Toc237307403"></a>
### **7\.1\.3 User Acceptance Testing \(UAT\)**

User Acceptance Testing checks whether a user can complete the task, not just whether the code runs\. As in Iteration 1 it was done through team scenario walkthroughs against the sprint backlog's acceptance criteria; Iteration 2 added the **driver** as a hands\-on user alongside the dispatcher\. The main scenarios were:

- A driver logs in on their phone, confirms an OTP if required, and the account binds to that device\.
- A driver moves their assigned trip through its phases in order, capturing the required evidence \(photos, seal numbers, signatures\) at each step\.
- A driver captures the seal at loading and confirms it at unloading; a mismatch is clearly flagged, not silently accepted\.
- A driver who briefly loses signal still completes a step; the action queues and sends automatically on reconnect, without duplication\.
- A driver raises an exception or panic/duress alert, and it is recorded even if the network is momentarily down\.
- A dispatcher sees trips update in real time as driver’s complete phases, without refreshing\.
- A dispatcher opens a trip and views its origin, destination, phase timeline and captured evidence\.
- A dispatcher can cancel a trip that must be aborted, or override a phase a driver cannot complete, with the reason recorded, so a trip is never left permanently stuck\.
- The system gives clear feedback on errors, an out\-of\-order action, a duplicate order number, an expired login, or an action on someone else's trip\.

Results were captured using test notes, API response logs, the automated test suite, and each story's acceptance criteria\.

<a id="_Toc237307404"></a>
### **7\.1\.4 Stress and Volume Testing**

Stress and volume testing remained limited in Iteration 2, because the iteration focused on building out the evidence chain and its correctness rather than on proving production capacity\. The purpose at this stage was to confirm the system behaves sensibly under basic repeated use and does not corrupt data\.

The areas considered for early stress and volume testing were:

- Multiple drivers completing phases at the same time across different trips\.
- Repeated viewing of trip, driver, and vehicle lists on the dashboard\.
- Batches of GPS location pings arriving frequently during transit\.
- Repeated or re\-sent "complete phase" and exception requests from the offline queue\.

# **8\. Development Approach and Scrum Application**

<a id="_Toc237307407"></a>
## **8\.1 Description of Development Approach**

Team05 applied Scrum through a prioritised Jira backlog, fixed\-length sprints, relative estimation, shared implementation ownership and regular inspection of delivered work\. This section describes the practices actually used during Iteration 2 rather than providing a theoretical overview of Scrum\.

<a id="_Toc237307408"></a>
### **8\.1\.1 Application of Scrum**

Iteration 2 comprised FP Sprint 3, FP Sprint 4 and FP Sprint 5\. Each sprint was scheduled for fourteen calendar days, allowing the team to compare cadence across the iteration\. Sprint 3 ran from 22 June to 6 July, Sprint 4 from 12 to 26 July, and Sprint 5 from 27 July to 10 August 2026\. Jira was used for backlog selection, ownership, workflow status and sprint reporting, while GitHub pull requests, commits, tests and migrations provided implementation evidence\.

The Scrum roles used in Iteration 1 were retained: Ciaran Formby acted as Product Owner, Tim Gultig acted as Scrum Master, and Ciaran Formby, Tim Gultig, Thomas Davis and Chiko Kasongo formed the Development Team\. Weekly online check\-ins were normally held on Wednesdays at approximately 11:00\. These meetings were used to discuss progress, integration decisions and impediments\. More frequent technical coordination took place through the repository and team communication channels when dependent backend and frontend work had to be aligned\.

<a id="_Toc237307409"></a>
### **8\.1\.2 Story\-Point Estimation and Planning**

User stories were represented using a relative Fibonacci\-style scale of 1, 2, 3, 5 and 8 points\. The team considered implementation complexity, uncertainty, external dependencies, testing effort and integration risk relative to previously completed stories\. Story points were attached to top\-level user stories; technical tasks were tracked separately so that task estimates did not inflate user\-story velocity\.

The Jira history shows that this practice was applied inconsistently during Iteration 2\. Sprint 3 had a usable starting baseline, while Sprint 5 began without committed scope and received its estimates and work items after the sprint had started\. The resulting Sprint 5 velocity is therefore reported for transparency but is not used as a reliable capacity forecast\. This was a result of teammates travelling and poor signal through the holiday\. But that did not effect the work done just the visual element on Jira\.

# **9\. Iteration 2 Status and Conclusion**

<a id="_Toc237307420"></a>
## **9\.1 Iteration 2 Progress Summary**

Iteration 2 ran from 22 June to 10 August 2026 across Sprints 3, 4 and 5\. The goal was to move FreightProof from a foundation to a working custody chain, and that goal was met\.

<a id="_Toc237307421"></a>
### **9\.1\.1 Completed Work**

- **Plan\-driven phase lifecycle**, replacing the fixed handshake sequence\. Phases are generated per trip and must be completed in order\.
- **Multi\-stop data model** \(TripStop, multiple consignments, per\-stop phase ordering\) in the database and backend\.
- **Driver PWA**: login with one\-time code, single\-device binding, phase walkthrough, photo and seal capture, exception and panic alerts\.
- **Offline queue** that replays actions once with an idempotency key when signal returns\.
- **Per\-phase Hedera anchoring** with SHA\-256 fingerprints, recorded anchor state and a fail\-safe policy\. No personal data goes on\-chain\.
- **Journey lock** protecting committed trip fields, so tampering is detectable\.
- **Evidence storage** in Supabase with hashing and short\-lived signed links scoped to the trip and organisation\.
- **Seal verification** comparing origin and destination seals automatically, and **geofencing** of the driver's device against the expected precinct\.
- **Dispatcher controls**: live event stream, phase timeline, admin\-only forensic view, trip cancellation and phase override with a logged reason\.
- **Automated testing**: pytest and Vitest unit tests plus integration tests against a disposable PostgreSQL database in CI\.

<a id="_Toc237307422"></a>
### **9\.1\.2 Partially Completed or Carried\-over Work**

- **Multi\-stop authoring\.** The backend supports variable\-length journeys, but the dispatcher still mostly creates a direct origin\-to\-destination trip\.
- **Parcel Perfect\.** Cargo is pulled at trip creation, but through mock and controlled data, because live access has not been granted\.
- **Receiver confirmation\.** Delivery is confirmed by a timestamped swipe, not by a captured name and ID number as the objective requires\.
- **Exception logging\.** Driver\-raised exceptions are permanent, but Pulsit route deviation alerts are not yet ingested\.
- **Anchoring speed\.** Anchoring works, but the 60 second target has not been measured under load\.
- **FP\-113 and FP\-124** were carried across two sprints and closed only in Sprint 5\.

## **9\.3 Conclusion and Forward View**

<a id="_Toc237307429"></a>
### **9\.3\.1 Iteration 2 Conclusion**

Iteration 2 delivered its scope after changing direction partway through\. The team began expecting a fixed handover sequence, found during implementation that real freight journeys do not work that way, and rebuilt the lifecycle around a generated phase plan\. That refactor cost most of the iteration's capacity but avoided a system that demonstrated well on a straight run and broke on anything else\.

Delivery was uneven\. Sprint 3 carried three items forward, Sprint 4 lost most of its capacity when a team member was unreachable for two weeks, and Sprint 5 absorbed both the backlog and the refactor\. Sprint 5 also started with no committed scope, so the reported average of 31\.33 points describes what happened rather than forecasting capacity\.

The honest limitation is that the evidence chain is complete in software but not yet corroborated externally\. Until Pulsit, IDVS and live Parcel Perfect are connected, the multi\-source verification that gives the evidence its weight rests mainly on the driver's own device\.

<a id="_Toc237307430"></a>
### **9\.3\.2 Priorities Moving Into Iteration 3**

1. Connect Pulsit, live Parcel Perfect and IDVS, closing the largest gap in the objectives\.
2. Finish multi\-stop authoring in the dispatcher interface\.
3. Bind evidence hashes into the anchored payload so files cannot be swapped without breaking verification\.
4. Capture receiver name and ID number at delivery confirmation\.
5. Harden security and stand up a deployed staging environment with the Driver PWA in production mode\.
6. Run the stress and volume scenarios and measure the 60 second anchoring target\.
7. Apply the ready\-before\-start rule and reconcile Jira continuously so Iteration 3 velocity is meaningful\.
