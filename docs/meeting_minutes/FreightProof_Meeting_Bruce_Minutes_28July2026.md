**0. Opening**

The call opened with informal greetings while participants joined and audio was set up. Ciaran confirmed everyone was present (Tom and Chiko named on the call) before starting.

Ciaran opened by thanking Bruce again for organising the RTT facility visit, noting that the visit had a major impact on the team's thinking — it prompted a restructuring/refactor of the system's data model and terminology. The purpose of this meeting was framed as a set of targeted follow-up questions to establish where Load Factor's own operation is the same as, or different from, what was observed at RTT.

**1. Load Factor's Role in the Supply Chain**

**1.1 Neutral Capacity Provider, Not a Competing Courier**

- Bruce clarified LFG's position in the chain. Courier/freight companies such as RTT or FedEx fulfil door-to-door collection and delivery (e.g. Johannesburg to Cape Town or Durban).
- Load Factor's role sits specifically in the **linehaul leg between hubs** — e.g. between RTT Johannesburg and RTT Durban. LFG supplies the hardware that fulfils that linehaul journey, either as airline capacity or as a vehicle.
- LFG is deliberately a **neutral player**. If LFG performed door-to-door delivery directly (e.g. for SA Blood Bank or MTN), it would be competing directly against its own customers (RTT, FedEx, etc.). Instead, LFG supplies vehicles and airline capacity to RTT, FedEx, and multiple other couriers who need capacity in that space.

**1.2 Protocols Follow the Origin Hub, with Client-Specific SLA Variation**

- Confirmed: LFG's vehicles and drivers operate within the entry/exit protocols of whichever hub/client they are servicing (RTT, FedEx, etc.) — broadly similar processes across clients.
- However, the specific SLA negotiated with each client can vary. Bruce referenced this variation had come up in a previous meeting.
- Importantly, Bruce stressed this does not materially change the process — it is fundamentally simple if two things are complied with: **entry/exit protocols** and **the deliverables** (e.g. supplying an 8-ton vehicle vs. 10 tons of airfreight capacity are different requirements with different rules).
- Example given: airfreight cargo must be sniffed by security dogs before being cleared, unlike a typical road-freight cross-dock — this is why the internal process at RTT's airfreight side worked slightly differently to a standard road cross-dock, and is governed by the SLA.

**2. Trip Structure: Incoming vs. Outgoing Vehicles**

**2.1 Every Departure Is a New Trip/Manifest**

- Ciaran proposed a model for clarification: that a truck arriving at a hub is recorded as part of the *previous* trip (the one that just delivered), and the *next* trip loaded onto that same truck does not have its own "arrival" record, because the truck is already on site.
- Bruce corrected this framing with a concrete example: LFG supplies an 8-ton vehicle to RTT out of Johannesburg; FedEx then turns that same vehicle around for a Durban→Johannesburg run. This is effectively the *opposite* of an "already-there truck starting fresh" — every vehicle/shipment that **departs** starts a **new trip**, on a new manifest basis, regardless of which direction it came from or who last used it.
- **Rule of thumb (Bruce's words):** the system needs to be able to read **both** an incoming and an outgoing vehicle event, but the manifest and its protocols are geared mainly toward the **export/departure side** — i.e. ex-Joburg or ex-Durban.
- Every vehicle/shipment that departs does so on a new trip. FreightProof will need the ability to **tie two trips together** — e.g. LFG supplies an 8-ton vehicle to RTT Durban and that same vehicle later needs to depart on a separate manifest. FreightProof can then see that RTT Durban is expecting that same vehicle, and the next manifest needs to record when it arrived.
- **Key clarification: the manifest is only generated by the branch sending the vehicle** (the departing branch), not the receiving branch.

**3. Driver Presence and Loading**

- Confirmed: as observed at RTT, the truck is often waiting to be loaded before the driver arrives — the driver is not always present with the truck at the start of loading, and typically arrives after loading is underway or complete.

**4. Back-to-Back Drivers and Duty-Time Regulation**

- Confirmed: it is common (Bruce estimated roughly 80% of the time on fixed contract routes, e.g. Joburg↔Durban) for a driver to do the return leg in the same truck once it is reloaded.
- Example: on a fixed Joburg–Durban–Joburg contract, the vehicle must depart Joburg by a set time (e.g. 1am latest); the driver stays over in Durban and returns the same vehicle at a scheduled time that evening (e.g. 8pm), i.e. the same driver does the return trip.
- Depending on the contract, LFG may instead employ a **Durban-based driver** so that, in his off time, he can go home, rest, shower, and return to drive the vehicle back — rather than one driver doing both legs back-to-back.
- **Time-and-duty regulation (legal constraint):** similar to pilots, a driver may not work continuously without rest beyond a defined period (Bruce referenced roughly 8 hours). For a short run like Durban, a single driver managing both legs may be feasible; for a longer run like Cape Town, the choice becomes either:
  - Two drivers on the vehicle (one drives while the other sleeps), or
  - An intermittent scheduled stopover (Bruce gave Colesberg as an example) where the driver rests before continuing.
- These scheduling patterns must be factored into trip planning **based on the specific contract**.
- **Cost implication (important):** running two drivers on a single trip means the trip must recover the cost of two drivers instead of one (Bruce gave an example driver salary of ~R30k/month) — this is a material cost driver in how a trip is resourced, not just an operational detail.

**5. "Container" Terminology Clarification**

- Ciaran raised that at RTT, Jacques had described "containers" within trucks, each with dedicated labels, and asked whether LFG has similar physical containers/sections, or just a load plan plus a seal.
- **Bruce clarified directly: what Jacques referred to as a "container" is 100% the same thing as a manifest — it is a virtual construct, not a physical container or section of the truck.**
- Example: on the Ethekwini (or similar) side, every night a shipment is sent through Load Factor, the "container" referred to for that night's shipment is literally the manifest reference (e.g. "LFG A001. [x]") — i.e. **container = manifest**, and it includes all the package/waybill labels within that manifest travelling that path.
- **This resolves Open Question #4 from the facility-visit findings** ("Container terminology") — confirmed: not a physical consolidation unit or shipping container, but a synonym for the manifest.

**6. Seal / Security Systems**

**6.1 Three Independent Seal/Security Events**

Bruce described LFG's security stack for a vehicle in transit as (typically) **three separate seal/verification events**:

1. **Pulsit geofence + biometric lock.** The vehicle is presupposed to be fitted with Pulsit. The vehicle's locking mechanism is controlled via a biometric system; if the vehicle goes outside its pre-agreed geofence range, it cannot be unlocked.
2. **Container (physical) seal / lock.** A band/lock goes around the two arms of the loading doors. The departing branch holds a key; the arriving branch holds the corresponding key, specific to that lock.
3. **Client seal number.** In addition to the above, LFG or the client (e.g. RTT) applies their own uniquely numbered seal.

- Which of the seal mechanisms apply on a given trip **depends on the client agreement** (e.g. RTT specifically may want their own numbered seal in addition to LFG's).
- Bruce confirmed the intent: ideally **all three events should be traceable and should reconcile** at the destination when the seals are broken — i.e. geofence status, container lock key, and seal number should all line up. Example given: RTT Joburg will know truck "BYD 123 GP" is arriving, that the geofence is active/will be unlocked, and that control room holds the container-lock keys plus the expected seal number to check against.
- **This resolves/substantially answers Open Question #3 from the facility-visit findings** ("Seal mechanism") for LFG's operation specifically — though it confirms multiple, client-dependent seal layers rather than a single mechanism, which the data model needs to accommodate (not assume exactly one seal event per trip).

**6.2 Pulsit / Geofence Data Availability**

- Ciaran asked whether the biometric/geofence data from Pulsit can be pulled into FreightProof, or whether that is still unknown.
- Bruce explained the operational process: before a vehicle departs (e.g. Joburg→Durban), the SLA agreement pre-defines the geographical geofence boundaries for that vehicle. If the vehicle moves outside those boundaries, it goes into "limp mode" automatically.
- At the initial signing of the SLA, **Pulsit is directly involved** in plotting the geofence areas. If an event occurs where the vehicle needs to deviate from its route (e.g. due to protests), **both control rooms — Pulsit's and RTT's (or the relevant client's)** — become actively involved.
- **Pulsit is described as the only company that can send the "receiver" command** (via GSM or similar) authorising the vehicle to exit and later re-enter the geofence — i.e. any authorised geofence deviation is actioned through Pulsit specifically, coordinated with the relevant control room.
- No firm confirmation yet on **API/data access** to pull this Pulsit data directly into FreightProof — this remains open (see Open Questions below), though the process by which geofence exceptions are handled is now understood.

**7. Waybill Destination — Meaning Confirmed**

- Ciaran asked directly: on a waybill, is the destination the *final* destination where the customer's parcel is actually delivered?
- **Bruce confirmed: yes, typically the RTT (or client) waybill destination is the final address** (e.g. an example address given: "Jet Park, corner of Springbok and Jones Road" through to "Cape Town Industrial").
- From LFG's perspective specifically: LFG consolidates many such waybills — each with its own final destination address — into a single **container/manifest**. LFG's own waybill for the hub-to-hub leg is a consolidation of these, since LFG performs hub-to-hub only, not final delivery.
- **This directly informs the earlier open modelling question on leg-scoped vs. journey-scoped destination:** the underlying client waybill (e.g. RTT's) does carry the true final destination; LFG's own leg-level document is a consolidation on top of that. Supports the leg-scoped recommendation from the phase-model design doc, with the true final destination sourced from the client's waybill data, not from LFG's leg manifest.

**8. Driver Trip Visibility & Scheduling Horizon**

- Ciaran asked how far in advance a driver knows about upcoming trips — e.g. can a driver see/plan for their next three or four trips, or is there a limit to how far ahead a dispatcher assigns a driver?
- Bruce split the answer into **two environments**:
  1. **Contractual/fixed routes:** e.g. RTT requires an 8-ton vehicle daily, Joburg→Durban. The driver and the rotation schedule know that driver is dedicated to that route for a fixed period (Bruce gave an example of two weeks) — this is known well in advance and is straightforward.
  2. **Ad hoc / on-demand bookings:** e.g. RTT has excess freight tonight and books an additional vehicle with LFG at short notice (Bruce gave an example of a 7pm booking for that same night). Assignment in this case is same-day/short-notice.
- Ciaran asked whether an ad hoc booking with only partial freight (e.g. not a full load) would be treated as an empty leg. **Bruce clarified: no — LFG's responsibility in that case is to find a backload** (e.g. with FedEx or Courier Guy) to fill the remaining capacity on the return or onward leg, rather than running it as an empty leg by default.

**9. Vehicle/Trailer Assignment Timing (Chiko's Question)**

- Chiko asked for clarification: when a trip is created (e.g. a week or a day in advance), is a **specific truck** (exact number plate/trailer) assigned at creation time, or only a truck **size/class**?
- **Bruce confirmed this happens on the day, not weeks in advance**, due to the mechanical/operational realities of the fleet (vehicles breaking down, etc.):
  - Typically, at a set time (Bruce gave the example of 12pm on the day), the client (e.g. RTT) creates the container/manifest for that trip.
  - LFG then confirms which vehicle — often the one that arrived the previous night — will be the one departing.
  - There is dynamic flexibility within this: the client may end up needing less capacity than originally planned (e.g. only a 4-tonner instead of a full 8-tonner), in which case LFG changes the vehicle assigned. The client then updates/corrects the manifest to reflect the trailer and horse actually being supplied.
  - **Confirmed explicitly: this reconciliation happens on the day, not weeks in advance**, even if the trip/booking itself was scheduled further ahead.
- This directly answers a system design question the team had: **a trip can be booked in advance, but the specific truck/trailer is only firmly assigned to that trip on the day** (or shortly before), reflecting real-time fleet availability and demand changes.
- Bruce tied this back to LFG's core value proposition: LFG's ability to **scale dynamically in the moment** is the reason the business model works. If a client like RTT ran its own fleet instead of using LFG, it would be 20–30% cheaper on paper, but the client would be committed to fixed assets and fixed drivers over time, losing the flexibility to respond to growth, demand changes, or client loss. Bruce confirmed this directly answered Tom's underlying question on the topic.
